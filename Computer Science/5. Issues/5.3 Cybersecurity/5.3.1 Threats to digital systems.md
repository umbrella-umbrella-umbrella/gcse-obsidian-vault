# 5.3.1 Threats to digital systems

## Cyberattacks

A **cyberattack** is a malicious act in which unauthorised people attack a computer system. The people responsible are often called **hackers**. Attacks may aim to steal information, damage an organisation, or encrypt data to demand money.

Stolen information can include email addresses, payment details, identity information, source code or patented designs. Stolen personal data may be used for identity theft or fraud. Attacks can cause financial losses, damage an organisation’s reputation, and lead to legal action.

### Examples of impact

- In 2017, the WannaCry ransomware attack affected hundreds of organisations. The NHS suffered an estimated £92 million loss and reputational damage. Many affected NHS trusts had not installed a security patch released by Microsoft.
- In 2020, attackers accessed high-profile verified Twitter accounts and posted messages asking people to send bitcoin. Twitter temporarily prevented verified accounts from posting while it investigated.
- In 2020, easyJet reported that email addresses and travel details belonging to approximately 9 million customers had been stolen. More than 2,000 customers also had credit card details stolen, and customers brought a class-action lawsuit.

## Malware

**Malware** is software that is hostile, malicious or intrusive. It can be used to steal or damage information, disrupt systems, or encrypt data for ransom.

Types of malware covered in this topic include:

- Viruses
- Worms
- Trojans
- Ransomware
- Keyloggers
- Spyware
- Adware
- Bots

## Hackers and vulnerabilities

A **hacker** is a person who attempts to gain, or gains, unauthorised access to a computer system. This may require technical knowledge, but attackers can also use social skills to manipulate people.

Possible motivations include:

- Financial gain, such as stealing bank details or identities
- Damaging an organisation
- Political motives
- Spying or corporate espionage
- Identifying vulnerabilities for an organisation
- Personal challenge or entertainment

A **software patch** is a security fix for existing software. Unpatched software can be an easy target because the vulnerabilities fixed by patches may already be publicly known or easy to discover. Keeping software patched reduces exposure to these known vulnerabilities.

## Social engineering

**Social engineering** is the use of deception or manipulation to exploit human behaviour, fallibility or goodwill and obtain confidential information or access. It targets people rather than computers.

Common tactics include:

| Tactic | How it is used |
|---|---|
| Phishing | Fraudulent messages try to trick people into revealing information or taking an unsafe action. |
| Pretexting (blagging) | An attacker invents a situation or identity to persuade someone to provide information or access. |
| Baiting | An attacker uses an enticing offer or item to tempt someone into an unsafe action. |
| Quid pro quo | An attacker offers a service or benefit in exchange for information or access. |

Examples of risky actions include opening a link in a suspicious account-warning email, plugging in a USB drive found in a public place, placing an untrusted download on a shared drive, or using personal social media on a work device against organisational rules.

## Ethical hacking

Not all hackers are malicious. **Ethical hackers** are authorised to test an organisation’s security and identify weaknesses. **Penetration testing** is the deliberate testing of a system, sometimes by attempting to break into it digitally or physically, to find vulnerabilities before malicious attackers do.
