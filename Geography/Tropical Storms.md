# Tropical Storms

HADLEY CELL – At the equator, the sun’s rays are more concentrated, so the air is warmer. This warm air rises, bringing low pressure (rain and clouds). When the air reacts around 30 degrees north/south, the sun’s rays are sparser, causing the air to be cooler, so it sinks, bringing high pressure (clear skies and no rain).
POLAR CELL – At the North/South poles, the sun’s rays are extremely sparse, causing the air to be very cold, making it sink – causing high pressure. When the air reaches 60-70 degrees north/south, the sun’s rays are more concentrated, causing the air to be warmer, so the air rises, bringing low pressure
FERREL CELL – in the wise words of Mr. S. Burton, “the Ferrel cell acts like a cog between the Hadley and polar cell, so once you have explained those just say it acts like a cog between them”.
First, there needs to be a thunderstorm over water which has a temperature of at least 27 degrees C. with low crosswinds, so the thunderstorm doesn’t get torn apart. Then, due to the Coriolis effect, the thunderstorm starts spinning, causing the eye and eye wall to form. It is now technically a tropical storm. When the tropical storm hits land, it brings heavy rainfall and storm surge. It then dissipates since it can’t get any energy from the sea.
They form during late summer/early autumn (like now – August-September usually)
They form anywhere from 5-20 degrees north/south, go west at first, then go north in the northern hemisphere and south in the southern hemisphere.
The intensity of tropical storms is predicted to increase due to climate change heating up the oceans. Climate change is also going to increase the number of places tropical storms can actually develop, including the south Atlantic, which previously had very few tropical storms form there however water temperatures have surpassed the 27-degree threshold there due to climate change.
The frequency of tropical storms may decrease.
When it comes to monitoring tropical storms key monitoring organizations include the National Hurricane Center in Florida and the Typhoon Warning Center in Hawaii. They track the wind speed; path the hurricane will take and predict the damage it will cause.
Hurricanes can change direction, so, a cone of uncertainty has to be made to cover the whole area a hurricane can technically go, so people know if their local area is at risk or not.
When it comes to reducing the effects of tropical storms, doing hurricane drills (like a fire drill but different – America has a lot of things they need to hold drills for like tornadoes earthquakes etc.) will help and children will know how to act in an emergency. HICs can also build storm shelters, which are raised in concrete to prevent them from flooding, and are made of concrete so they cannot be destroyed by the strong winds or any objects the hurricane is throwing at it. However, in LICs (like Hittesh’s homeland, Sri Lanka) this isn’t an option as they are pretty expensive.
Also, in HICs houses in high-risk areas, Florida can be built featuring stormproof windows and tiles, so the house can remain intact through a hurricane, lowering damage and improving the area’s hurricane resistance. Again, this simply isn’t possible in LICs as they are expensive.
