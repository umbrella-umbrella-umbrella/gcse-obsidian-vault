# Tropical Storms

## Hadley Cell
At the equator, the sun’s rays are more concentrated, so the air is warmer. This warm air rises, bringing low pressure (rain and clouds). When the air reacts around 30 degrees north/south, the sun’s rays are sparser, causing the air to be cooler, so it sinks, bringing high pressure (clear skies and no rain).
## Polar Cell
At the North/South poles, the sun’s rays are extremely sparse, causing the air to be very cold, making it sink – causing high pressure. When the air reaches 60-70 degrees north/south, the sun’s rays are more concentrated, causing the air to be warmer, so the air rises, bringing low pressure
## Ferrel Cell
The Ferrel cell circulates between the Hadley and Polar cells.
## Tropical Storm Formation
Tropical storms form when there is a thunderstorm over water which has a temperature of at least 27 degrees C. with low crosswinds, so the thunderstorm doesn’t get torn apart. Then, due to the Coriolis effect, the thunderstorm starts spinning, causing the eye and eye wall to form. It is now technically a tropical storm. When the tropical storm hits land, it brings heavy rainfall and storm surge, then dissipates since it can’t get any energy from the sea.
- They usually form during late summer and early autumn, often August–September.
- They form between 5° and 20° north and south of the equator. They initially move west, then turn poleward.
Climate change is expected to increase tropical storm intensity and expand the areas where storms can form as ocean temperatures rise.
The frequency of tropical storms may decrease.
## Monitoring
Key monitoring organizations include the National Hurricane Center in Florida and the Typhoon Warning Center in Hawaii. They track wind speed and storm paths to predict the damage it will cause.
Hurricanes can change direction, so a cone of uncertainty has to be made to cover the whole area a hurricane may travel, so people know if their local area is at risk or not.
## Reducing Impacts
Hurricane drills help and children will know how to act in an emergency. HICs can also build storm shelters, which are raised in concrete to prevent them from flooding, and are made of concrete so they cannot be destroyed by the strong winds or any windborne debris. However, in LICs this isn’t an option because they are expensive.
Also, in HICs houses in high-risk areas, Florida can be built featuring stormproof windows and tiles, so the house can remain intact through a hurricane, lowering damage and improving the area’s hurricane resistance. These features may be too expensive for LICs.
