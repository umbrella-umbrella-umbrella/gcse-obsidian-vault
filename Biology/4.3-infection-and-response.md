## Pathogens and communicable disease


Pathogens are microorganisms that cause infectious disease. They include viruses, bacteria, protists and fungi and can infect plants or animals. Spread can occur by direct contact, water or air. Bacteria and viruses reproduce rapidly in the body; bacteria may release toxins that damage tissues. Viruses reproduce inside cells, damaging them.

### Specified examples


- Measles (virus): spread in droplets from coughs and sneezes; causes fever and red rash and may be fatal if complications occur. Most young children are vaccinated.
- HIV (virus): initially flu-like; spread through sexual contact or exchange of body fluids such as blood. It attacks immune cells; untreated late-stage infection (AIDS) leaves the body unable to deal with other infections or cancers. Antiretroviral drugs can control HIV.
- Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV): plant virus causing mosaic leaf discolouration; reduced photosynthesis affects growth.
- Salmonella (bacterium): ingested in contaminated food or food prepared unhygienically; causes fever, cramps, vomiting and diarrhoea due to bacteria and toxins. Poultry vaccination helps control spread.
- Gonorrhoea (bacterium): sexually transmitted; causes thick yellow/green discharge and pain urinating. Antibiotic resistance has developed. Spread is reduced by antibiotic treatment and barrier contraception such as condoms.
- Rose black spot (fungus): purple/black leaf spots; leaves yellow and drop early, reducing photosynthesis and growth. Spread by wind or water; fungicides and removal/destruction of affected leaves can control it.
- Malaria (protist): life cycle includes mosquitoes; recurrent fever and can be fatal. Control mosquito breeding and use nets to prevent bites.

## Human defences and vaccination


- Skin acts as a barrier.
- Nose hairs and mucus trap particles and pathogens.
- Mucus in trachea and bronchi traps pathogens; cilia move mucus away from lungs.
- Stomach acid kills many pathogens swallowed in food or mucus.
- White blood cells destroy pathogens by phagocytosis, produce antibodies specific to antigens, and produce antitoxins that neutralise toxins.
Vaccination introduces small quantities of dead or inactive pathogen to stimulate white blood cells to make antibodies. On later exposure, white blood cells respond rapidly and produce the correct antibodies, preventing illness. Immunising a large proportion of the population reduces pathogen spread. Schedules and specific vaccine side effects are not required.

## Medicines, antibiotics and drug development


Antibiotics (for example penicillin) kill bacteria inside the body; the correct antibiotic is needed. Antibiotics do not kill viruses. Painkillers treat symptoms but do not kill pathogens. Antiviral drugs are difficult to develop because viruses reproduce inside body cells, so a drug may damage host tissues.

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria survive treatment and reproduce, so resistant strains become more common. Antibiotics should be used appropriately to slow this process.

Drugs have historically come from plants and microorganisms: digitalis from foxgloves, aspirin from willow, and penicillin from Penicillium mould. Many drugs are now synthesised, sometimes starting from plant chemicals. New drugs are tested for toxicity (harm), efficacy (whether they work) and dose.

1. Preclinical tests in laboratories use cells, tissues and live animals.
2. Clinical trials involve healthy volunteers and patients. Start with very low doses; if safe, further trials identify an optimum dose.
3. Double-blind trials give some participants a placebo; neither participant nor treating researcher knows who receives which treatment, reducing bias. Results are scrutinised through peer review before publication.

## Monoclonal antibodies (Biology only, HT only)


Monoclonal antibodies are identical antibodies from a single clone of cells. Each is specific to one binding site on one antigen. Stimulate mouse lymphocytes to make a target antibody, fuse them with tumour cells to make hybridoma cells, select and clone a single hybridoma, then collect and purify the antibody.

- Diagnosis: pregnancy tests; measuring hormones or chemicals; detecting pathogens.
- Research: attach a fluorescent dye to locate specific molecules in cells or tissues.
- Treatment: attach a radioactive substance, toxic drug or cell-growth inhibitor to target cancer cells.
They can target a specific molecule or cell, but have caused more side effects than expected and are not as widely used as originally hoped. Specific test and treatment details are not required; apply information supplied in a question.

## Plant disease, detection and defence (Biology only)


Plant disease signs include stunted growth, leaf spots, decay/rot, growths, malformed stems or leaves, discolouration and pests. HT: identify disease using a gardening manual or website, laboratory diagnosis, or testing kits containing monoclonal antibodies. Specified pathogens include TMV (virus), black spot (fungus) and aphids (insects).

- Nitrate deficiency causes stunted growth because nitrate ions are needed for amino acids/protein synthesis and growth.
- Magnesium deficiency causes chlorosis because magnesium ions are needed to make chlorophyll.
- Physical defences: cellulose cell walls, tough waxy cuticle, and layers of dead cells (bark) that fall off.
- Chemical defences: antibacterial chemicals and poisons that deter herbivores.
- Mechanical/adaptive defences: thorns and hairs, leaves that droop or curl when touched, and mimicry that tricks animals.
