## Cell types and sub-cellular structures

### Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells


- Eukaryotic cells (animal and plant cells) have genetic material enclosed in a nucleus, plus cytoplasm and a cell membrane.
- Prokaryotic bacterial cells are much smaller. They have cytoplasm and a cell membrane surrounded by a cell wall; their DNA is not enclosed in a nucleus. Their genetic material is one DNA loop, and they may contain small DNA rings called plasmids.

### Structures and functions


- Nucleus: contains chromosomes made of DNA; genes on chromosomes carry information.
- Cell membrane: controls movement of substances into and out of the cell.
- Cytoplasm: where most of the cell chemical reactions take place.
- Mitochondria: where aerobic respiration occurs.
- Ribosomes: where proteins are made.
- Plant cell wall: cellulose; strengthens and supports the cell.
- Chloroplasts: contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light for photosynthesis.
- Permanent vacuole: contains cell sap and helps keep the cell rigid.
- Bacterial plasmids: small rings of DNA separate from the main DNA loop.

### Specialised cells


- Sperm cell: tail helps it swim; many mitochondria provide energy; acrosome contains enzymes; one set of chromosomes.
- Nerve cell: long extensions carry impulses over distances; branched ends connect to other cells; insulating sheath increases impulse transmission speed.
- Muscle cell: contractile fibres shorten; many mitochondria supply energy for contraction.
- Root hair cell: long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport.
- Xylem cell: hollow, strengthened tube transports water and mineral ions.
- Phloem cell: elongated living cells form tubes that transport dissolved sugars.

### Differentiation and stem cells


Differentiation is the process by which a cell changes to become specialised. Most animal cells differentiate early in development; in mature animals, cell division mainly repairs and replaces cells. Many plant cells retain the ability to differentiate throughout life.

A stem cell is an undifferentiated cell that can divide to make more cells of the same type and can produce certain other cell types by differentiation. Embryonic stem cells can form most human cell types; adult bone marrow stem cells can form several types, including blood cells; plant meristems can form any plant cell type.

Therapeutic cloning makes an embryo with the patient’s genes, so its stem cells may be less likely to be rejected. Potential benefits include replacing damaged cells (for example, in diabetes or paralysis). Risks include transfer of viral infection; ethical and religious objections are also raised. Meristem cells can rapidly produce plant clones, including rare species and crop plants with useful features. Detailed stem-cell techniques are not required by this specification.

### Microscopy and scale


Electron microscopes have greater magnification and resolving power than light microscopes, revealing finer sub-cellular detail. Magnification is how many times larger an image is than the real object; resolution is the ability to distinguish two close points as separate.

Magnification = image size / real size. Rearrange: image size = magnification x real size; real size = image size / magnification. Convert units before calculation: 1 mm = 1,000 um; 1 um = 1,000 nm. Use standard form when appropriate and include units.

### Cell cycle and mitosis


1. The cell grows and increases the number of sub-cellular structures, such as mitochondria and ribosomes.
2. The DNA replicates, making two copies of each chromosome.
3. During mitosis, one set of chromosomes is pulled to each end and the nucleus divides. The cytoplasm and cell membrane then divide, producing two genetically identical cells.
Mitosis is used for growth and development in multicellular organisms and for repair and replacement. Learn the three overall stages; named phases within mitosis are not required.

### Culturing microorganisms (Biology only)


Bacteria reproduce by binary fission and may divide about every 20 minutes under suitable conditions with enough nutrients. They can grow in nutrient broth or as colonies on agar. Uncontaminated cultures are needed to test disinfectants and antibiotics.

- Sterilise Petri dishes and growth medium to kill unwanted microorganisms.
- Sterilise the inoculating loop by passing it through a flame before transfer.
- Open the dish lid as little as possible to reduce contamination; secure it with adhesive tape and incubate upside down so condensation does not drip onto the culture.
- In school laboratories, incubate at 25 C to reduce the chance of growing harmful pathogens.
Population calculation: number of divisions = time / mean division time. For binary fission, population = starting population x 2^(number of divisions). HT: express very large answers in standard form. Colony or inhibition-zone area = pi x r squared. Measure the clear-zone radius consistently; a larger clear zone indicates more inhibition under those test conditions.

### Required practical 1: microscopy of plant and animal cells


1. Prepare a thin specimen on a slide; add an appropriate stain when needed to improve contrast.
2. Lower a coverslip carefully to avoid trapping air.
3. Place the slide on the microscope stage. Start on low power, focus, then use higher power as required.
4. Observe and draw selected cells, label visible structures, and include a magnification scale.
Good practice: use a sharp pencil, clear single lines, no shading, and accurate labels. Record magnification or scale as instructed. Compare relative sizes only when images are at a comparable scale.

### Required practical 2: effect of antiseptics or antibiotics on bacterial growth


1. Use aseptic technique to inoculate sterile agar with the bacterial culture and prepare sterile paper discs containing different antiseptics or antibiotics. Include a control disc.
2. Place discs onto the agar using sterile forceps, secure the Petri-dish lid with tape and incubate inverted at 25 C as specified for school laboratories.
3. Measure the diameter (or radius) of each clear zone of inhibition. Calculate area with pi x r squared if needed.
4. Repeat and compare means. A larger clear zone indicates greater inhibition in these conditions; compare like-for-like concentrations and disc sizes.
Keep bacterial species, culture density, agar depth, disc size, concentration and incubation time consistent. Sterilise equipment and avoid opening dishes after incubation; follow local safety and disposal instructions.

## Transport across cell membranes

### Diffusion, osmosis and active transport


- Diffusion: net movement of particles from higher to lower concentration. Rate increases with a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature and larger membrane surface area. Examples include oxygen and carbon dioxide exchange and urea moving from cells into blood plasma.
- Osmosis: net diffusion of water from a dilute solution to a more concentrated solution through a partially permeable membrane.
- Active transport: movement from a more dilute solution to a more concentrated solution, against the concentration gradient; it requires energy from respiration. Examples: mineral ions entering root hair cells from dilute soil; sugars absorbed from the gut into blood when blood concentration is higher.
Exchange surfaces are needed because multicellular organisms have a small surface area compared with volume and a high demand for exchange. Effective surfaces have a large area and thin membrane; animals also have an efficient blood supply, and gas-exchange surfaces are ventilated. Examples include small intestine and lungs in mammals, fish gills, plant roots and leaves.

### Surface area to volume ratio


Surface area to volume ratio = surface area / volume. Calculate both in consistent units before dividing. Smaller objects usually have a larger surface area to volume ratio, allowing faster exchange relative to their volume. Large multicellular organisms need specialised exchange surfaces and transport systems.

### Required practical 3: osmosis in plant tissue


1. Cut equal-sized pieces of plant tissue and measure each initial mass. Keep tissue type, dimensions, blotting and weighing consistent.
2. Place pieces in a range of known salt or sugar concentrations for the same time. Use repeats for each concentration.
3. Remove, gently blot dry consistently, and measure final mass. Calculate change or percentage change.
4. Calculate the mean for each concentration and plot concentration against mean percentage change in mass.
Percentage change = (final mass - initial mass) / initial mass x 100. A gain means water entered the tissue; a loss means water left. At the concentration with no net change, solution and cell contents are approximately isotonic. Control time, temperature, tissue dimensions and solution volume.
