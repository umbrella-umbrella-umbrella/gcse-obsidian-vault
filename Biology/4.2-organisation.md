## Levels of organisation and enzymes


Cells form tissues (groups of cells with similar structure and function); tissues combine into organs; organs work together in organ systems; organ systems make an organism.

Enzymes are biological catalysts. Their specific active site has a shape complementary to a particular substrate (lock-and-key model). The substrate binds, the reaction occurs, and products leave; the enzyme is unchanged and reusable. Increasing temperature raises particle movement and collision rate up to the optimum. Above the optimum, the active site changes shape (denatures), so the substrate no longer fits. A pH change can alter the active site and reduce activity; extreme pH can denature an enzyme.

Rate = quantity of product formed / time, or quantity of reactant used / time. For a fixed endpoint, rate is often compared as 1 / time. State units and use the same endpoint for fair comparisons.

## Digestive system and digestion


Digestion breaks large insoluble food molecules into small soluble molecules that can be absorbed into the bloodstream. Enzymes catalyse these reactions.

- Carbohydrases break carbohydrates into simple sugars. Amylase, made in salivary glands and pancreas, breaks starch into sugars.
- Proteases break proteins into amino acids. They are produced in the stomach, pancreas and small intestine.
- Lipases break lipids into glycerol and fatty acids. They are produced in the pancreas and small intestine.
- Bile is made in the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It neutralises stomach hydrochloric acid (it is alkaline) and emulsifies fat into small droplets. This increases surface area and provides alkaline conditions, increasing lipase rate.

### Required practical 4: food tests


- Reducing sugar: add Benedict’s solution and heat in a hot water bath. Positive: blue changes through green/yellow/orange to brick-red precipitate.
- Starch: add iodine solution. Positive: orange-brown to blue-black.
- Protein: add Biuret reagent. Positive: blue to lilac/purple.
- Lipids: emulsion test: add ethanol and shake, then add water; a milky-white emulsion indicates lipid.
Use clean labelled samples and the same reagent amount. The specification explicitly names Benedict’s, iodine and Biuret; lipid testing is included in the range of tests. Follow teacher instructions for safe handling and heating.

### Required practical 5: pH and amylase


1. Prepare starch and amylase mixtures with buffer solutions at a range of pH values. Keep volumes and concentrations consistent.
2. Control temperature using a water bath or electric heater; allow solutions to reach target temperature.
3. Mix amylase and starch and start timing. Every 30 seconds, place a drop of reaction mixture onto iodine.
4. Record the time when iodine no longer turns blue-black; all starch has been digested.
5. Compare rate using 1 / time, or compare times to the same endpoint; plot pH against rate.
Independent variable: pH. Dependent variable: time for starch to disappear (or calculated rate). Control temperature, concentrations, volumes and sampling interval. Use repeats and a consistent iodine test.

## Circulation, heart, lungs and blood


The heart pumps blood through a double circulatory system. The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs; the left ventricle pumps blood to the rest of the body. The right atrium contains the natural pacemaker, a group of cells controlling resting heart rate. Artificial pacemakers correct irregularities.

Heart vessels to know: vena cava brings deoxygenated blood from body to heart; pulmonary artery takes it to lungs; pulmonary vein returns oxygenated blood from lungs; aorta carries oxygenated blood to body; coronary arteries supply heart muscle. Valve names are not required.

Lungs: trachea branches into bronchi, which branch into smaller airways ending in alveoli. Alveoli have large surface area, thin walls and a surrounding capillary network for rapid gas exchange. Ventilation maintains concentration gradients.

### Blood vessels


- Arteries carry blood away from the heart at high pressure; thick muscular and elastic walls withstand pressure.
- Veins return blood to the heart at lower pressure; thinner walls and a wide lumen reduce resistance; valves prevent backflow.
- Capillaries connect arteries and veins; their very thin walls allow exchange with tissues.

### Blood components


- Plasma transports dissolved substances, including carbon dioxide, urea, hormones and digested food, as well as blood cells.
- Red blood cells carry oxygen; they have no nucleus, a biconcave shape and contain haemoglobin.
- White blood cells defend against pathogens by phagocytosis and by producing antibodies and antitoxins.
- Platelets are cell fragments that help blood clot, reducing blood loss and pathogen entry.

## Health, cardiovascular disease and cancer


Health is a state of physical and mental well-being. Communicable and non-communicable disease, diet, stress and life circumstances affect health. Diseases can interact: immune defects increase infection risk; viruses in cells can trigger cancers; immune reactions can trigger allergies; serious physical illness can affect mental health.

### Coronary heart disease and treatment


In coronary heart disease, fatty material builds up inside coronary arteries, narrowing them and reducing oxygenated blood flow to heart muscle. Stents hold narrowed arteries open. Statins lower blood cholesterol and slow fatty deposit build-up.

- Stents restore blood flow but involve a procedure and risks such as complications or clotting.
- Statins reduce cholesterol and future risk, but may have side effects and need ongoing use.
- Faulty valves may not open fully or may leak, impairing blood flow. Replacement valves can be mechanical or biological, each with advantages and disadvantages.
- For heart failure, donor heart or heart-and-lung transplant may be used. Donor organs are scarce and rejection is a risk. Artificial hearts can keep a patient alive while waiting or let the heart rest, but can cause bleeding, infection or clotting and require power/control equipment.
Evaluate treatments by balancing benefit, risk, availability, cost and quality of life, using information in the question.

### Lifestyle and risk factors


A risk factor is something associated with an increased rate of disease. It may be a lifestyle factor or substance in the body or environment. Association alone does not prove causation; a causal mechanism is established for some risks but not all. Many diseases reflect interacting factors.

- Diet, smoking and exercise affect cardiovascular disease risk.
- Obesity is a risk factor for Type 2 diabetes.
- Alcohol can harm liver and brain function; alcohol and smoking can harm unborn babies.
- Smoking increases risk of lung disease and lung cancer.
- Carcinogens, including ionising radiation, are cancer risk factors.
Questions may ask for human and financial costs at individual, community, national or global scale. Epidemiological data require representative sampling and careful interpretation. Correlation does not on its own prove causation.

### Cancer


Cancer results from changes in cells leading to uncontrolled growth and division. Benign tumours remain in one place, usually within a membrane, and do not invade other body parts. Malignant tumours invade neighbouring tissues and can spread in blood to form secondary tumours. Lifestyle and genetic risk factors are known for some cancers.

## Plant tissues and transport


- Epidermal tissue covers and protects plant surfaces.
- Palisade mesophyll is near the upper leaf surface and specialised for photosynthesis.
- Spongy mesophyll has air spaces that support gas exchange.
- Xylem transports water and mineral ions from roots to stems and leaves; hollow tubes strengthened with lignin form the transpiration stream.
- Phloem transports dissolved sugars from leaves to the rest of the plant for use or storage. This movement is translocation. Phloem consists of elongated cells with pores between them; detailed structure and transport mechanism are not required.
- Meristem tissue at shoot and root tips contains cells that can differentiate.
- Guard cells around stomata control gas exchange and water loss.
Root hair cells absorb water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport. Transpiration is water loss from leaves by evaporation and diffusion through stomata; it creates a pull moving water up xylem. Higher temperature, greater air movement and light intensity usually increase transpiration; higher humidity reduces it by reducing the water-vapour gradient.

Practical-linked skills include measuring water uptake with a potometer as an estimate of transpiration, investigating stomatal distribution, taking repeats, calculating means and interpreting graphs. A potometer measures water uptake, used as an estimate because not all absorbed water is transpired.
